About

I study how people, organisations and economies respond to public policy, enforcement, risk and economic shocks, using laboratory experiments and applied econometrics.

I am a Lecturer in Economics at the University of Essex, where I am also completing my PhD in Economics. My research sits at the intersection of behavioural and experimental economics and public policy evaluation, with applications to tax compliance, financial formalisation, collective action and local governance.

My route into economics ran through civil engineering, commercial banking at the State Bank of India and public administration. Before Essex, I was an Assistant Professor at St Berchmans College, where I taught across mathematical, behavioural, micro- and macroeconomics and econometrics, and led curriculum development.

September 2026: Joined the Department of Economics at the University of Essex as Lecturer in Economics.

Liquidity under Constraint: Experimental Evidence on Behavioural Adaptation to Sudden Monetary Shocks

Richie Dilip Kurian · 2026

Tax complianceDemonetisationLoss aversionLab experiment
Abstract

Motivated by India's 2016 demonetisation, this paper designs a multi-round laboratory tax-compliance experiment with a built-in liquidity shock, comparing a no-shock control with pre-announced and sudden shocks. Both shocks raise deposits and reduce cash spending while restrictions are in force. Cash spending returns towards its earlier level once restrictions end, but deposit behaviour persists, particularly after a sudden shock and among participants with prior audit experience. The design separates genuine compliance gains from a mechanical shift of spending into formal channels.

Cooperation under Escalating Risk: Evidence from a Dynamic Public Goods Experiment

with Friederike Mengel and Dennie van Dolder · 2026

Public goodsCollective riskGrowth biasLab experiment
Abstract

We introduce a framework for studying cooperation when the probability of collective failure rises with group behaviour, varying initial risk, contribution effectiveness, risk growth and group size. Smaller groups adapt more effectively to escalating risk, while larger groups are more sensitive to the pace of risk growth and to incentive design. Individual differences in exponential-growth bias and probability bias explain much of the variation in contribution timing.

The Effect of Brexit on the UK and EU: A Synthetic Control Analysis

with Neslihan Sakarya · 2026

Synthetic controlBrexitConformal inference
Abstract

Using quarterly real GDP from 1960 to 2024, we build synthetic control counterfactuals for the UK and major EU economies, separating Brexit from shocks such as COVID-19. Effects on the UK are concentrated around implementation rather than the referendum, with a modest net cumulative effect by 2024, while spillovers to Germany and France are broadly muted.

Behavioural Barriers to Digital and Financial Formalisation among Informal Microentrepreneurs in India

with Pavanam Thomas · 2026

Financial inclusionInformalityNudgesSEM
Abstract

Why does digital-payment adoption stay low among informal microenterprises despite India's digital public infrastructure? Combining surveys, incentivised behavioural tasks and randomised nudges, the study tests how risk and loss aversion, present bias, complexity aversion, institutional mistrust, hassle costs and social norms shape formalisation decisions.
